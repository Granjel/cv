# Linking plant traits to species interactions and coexistence under global change

Functional traits shape how plants acquire light, water and nutrients. These traits vary within and among species and may help explain which plants coexist as conditions change. I expect species with greater overlap in resource-use traits to compete more strongly, while differences in traits such as height may predict which competitor has the advantage. With Carlos Pérez Carmona’s TraitLab at the Misión Biológica de Galicia (MBG-CSIC), I will link individual trait measurements to observed interactions and estimate niche and fitness differences from population data. I will test how these relationships vary across years, along land-use gradients and under experimental warming.

I bring long-term records from the Doñana marshes and data on plant interactions from the PaNDiv grassland experiment in Switzerland. At the Basque Centre for Climate Change, I use Bayesian population models to study how plant communities recover after disturbance in a warming experiment in France. At MBG, I will learn from Carmona and Pablo Castro Sánchez-Bermejo to map the combinations of leaf and root traits found among individuals of each species. The maps show which combinations are common and where species overlap. My contribution will be to estimate niche and fitness differences from population data, a new approach for TraitLab. Together we can test whether these patterns explain the interactions we measure in the field.

I will combine my data with experiments in China led by Jing Yang, who studies competition among subtropical tree seedlings with Carmona. They found that traits predicted growth better for seedlings grown alone than for those grown with neighbours. I will use the traits of both plants to predict how strongly each affects its neighbour’s growth. Do species with similar leaves compete less strongly when their roots differ? Where population data allow, I will estimate whether a weaker competitor can persist and how much that prediction changes across the likely range of interaction strengths. Data from sites or years left out of the models will provide an independent test. With individual plant measurements from TraitDivNet and INDIGO, I will examine how trait combinations vary across environments worldwide. Placing these records on the same map will show where else the results might apply.

During a three-month stay at the University of Tartu, I will learn from Meelis Pärtel, Junichi Fujinuma and Riin Tamme to estimate which species in the region could grow at a site but are locally absent. These species make up its dark diversity. Using the results from MBG, I will simulate what happens when a few individuals of one of these species arrive. Can their population grow alongside the plants already there? I will repeat the test for resident species, treating each in turn as rare. For both groups I will use the range of interaction strengths predicted by their traits. This will test whether suitable species are more often missing where they are unlikely to overcome competition from resident plants. If a missing species is predicted to establish, the next question is whether it can reach the site.

The work at MBG and Tartu will test how far plant traits can explain both the species we find together and those missing from suitable sites. I will lead the study and share the models and code. This will establish my independent research line on how warming and land use change which plants persist in natural and human-modified communities.

**Keywords**: Plant coexistence; Functional traits; Species interactions; Dark diversity; Environmental change
