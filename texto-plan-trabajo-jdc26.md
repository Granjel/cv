# Linking functional traits to the mechanisms of plant coexistence in changing environments

Functional traits shape how plants acquire light, water and nutrients. These traits vary within and among species and may explain which plants can coexist as conditions change. Species with similar resource-use traits may compete more strongly because their niches are more similar, while differences in traits such as height may give one species a competitive advantage. With Carlos P. Carmona’s TraitLab at the Misión Biológica de Galicia (MBG-CSIC), I will link individual trait measurements to observed interactions and estimate coexistence mechanisms, namely niche and fitness differences, from population data. I will test how these relationships vary across years, along land-use gradients and under experimental warming.

As a Juan de la Cierva fellow, I will bring to TraitLab the population models and datasets I use to estimate niche and fitness differences. During my doctoral and postdoctoral work, I contributed to long-term vegetation monitoring in the Doñana marshes and measured plant interactions in the PaNDiv grassland experiment in Switzerland. At the Basque Centre for Climate Change, I use Bayesian population models to study how plant communities recover after disturbance in a warming experiment in France. In turn, I will learn from Carmona and postdoc Pablo Castro Sánchez-Bermejo how to build probabilistic trait spaces from individual plant measurements and use them to compare neighbouring species, for example by quantifying how often thin leaves and fine roots occur together in each.

I will work with postdoc Jing Yang, whose plant competition experiments in China measured growth with and without neighbours. By combining her experiments with my datasets, we will test when overlap in leaf and root traits predicts smaller niche differences, and whether environmental change affects the relationship between functional traits and competitive advantage. For example, if a combination of leaf and root traits gives a species an advantage under warming, how common is that combination among plants at warmer sites? For this, I will use individual plant measurements from Carmona's extensive TraitDivNet and INDIGO trait datasets to test for such patterns across communities worldwide.

During a three-month stay at the University of Tartu, I will learn from Professor Meelis Pärtel and researchers Junichi Fujinuma and Riin Tamme to estimate an ecosystem’s dark diversity, i.e. regional species that are locally absent despite having traits suited to the site. Using the trait–coexistence links from MBG-CSIC, I will simulate whether resident and dark species can invade when rare across the range of interaction strengths their traits predict. This will test whether species are more likely to be absent from suitable sites where niche differences cannot offset their predicted competitive disadvantage.

My work at TraitLab and Tartu will test how far plant traits can explain the coexistence mechanisms between both the species we find together and those missing from suitable sites. I will lead at least two papers and openly share the data and code needed to test our predictions in other communities. I will use these results to develop Ramón y Cajal and ERC Starting Grant proposals for an independent research programme on how species interactions shape plant communities as environments change.

**Keywords**: Plant coexistence, Functional traits, Species interactions, Dark diversity, Environmental change
